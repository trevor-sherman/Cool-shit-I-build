Personal Project · Spring 2026

Autonomous Underwater Vehicle

A low-cost autonomous submarine built to follow a preset path and collect data about the water around it. It dives with stepper-driven ballast tanks, steers with four servo-actuated fins, and runs on a custom 24 V battery pack inside a sealed clear PVC hull. A tethered surface buoy with GPS and a radio tracks its position and links it to shore.

The goal

Build a fully autonomous submarine that can travel along a preset path and gather information about its environment. It's designed as a flexible platform: the nose is a large, open payload bay, so the same vehicle can carry different sensors and equipment for different missions, including:

  • Environmental monitoring, such as water quality and pollutant testing
  • Ecosystem and species research
  • Defense applications

The project runs in three phases: first the vehicle itself, then autonomy, then sensing, data logging, and transmission. Phase 1, the submarine, is the focus of this page.

Key design decisions

Communication

Water absorbs radio energy, and the higher the frequency, the faster the signal dies. A signal's skin depth, δ = √(2ρ / ωμ), is the distance at which it falls to 37% of its strength. WiFi and Bluetooth at 2.4 GHz barely make it a few centimeters through water. Military submarines get around this with very low and extremely low frequency radio, but at very slow data rates.

I compared three approaches: a tether to a floating radio buoy, low-frequency radio (27, 40, or 75 MHz, traditionally used by RC submarines), and the 900 MHz and 2.4 GHz bands. The low bands penetrate water best, but hardware for them is no longer being made, and they can't carry enough data for telemetry. I went with a tethered buoy. A long cable runs from the sub to a small float on the surface, which carries an NRF24L01 2.4 GHz radio and a NEO-6M GPS module. The radio stays above the water, so the link is reliable and can use cheap, widely supported 2.4 GHz hardware with plenty of bandwidth for telemetry. Because the buoy follows the sub, its GPS also gives the sub's position for navigation. The tradeoffs are that depth is limited by the tether length, and the cable could snag on debris or tangle in the propeller.

Diving

Dynamic diving uses fins and forward speed to push the vehicle down. It's simple and fails safe, but the sub can only stay under while it's moving. Static diving uses ballast tanks to change buoyancy, so the sub can sink, rise, and hold a depth without moving. I chose static diving with ballast tanks for depth control, plus fins for steering underway.

What I did

I started the project and designed the vehicle in Onshape, then built and tested it.

Layout

The sub has three main sections. The nose is a hollow, 3D-printed, streamlined shell with a large bay for future sensors. The watertight cylinder in the middle holds the batteries, ballast tanks, and electronics, and is the only dry part of the vehicle. The tail holds the four fin servos and the main thruster. Everything outside the cylinder is a wet hull, designed to flood. The 3D-printed parts outside the cylinder are printed with gyroid infill, whose open, connected cells let water fill them completely instead of trapping air.

A 3D-printed outer shell slides over the cylinder. It protects the clear tube from rocks and debris, keeps sunlight out so the electronics stay cooler, and is the structure that holds the whole vehicle together.

Watertight cylinder

The hull is a 3 ft length of 4″ inside-diameter clear schedule 40 PVC pipe. All of the internals mount on two 3 ft, 6 mm aluminum rods, the same approach used for avionics bays in rocketry and common in RC submarines. The batteries, ballast tanks, and electronics slide onto the rails and lock in place with shaft collars, and the whole bay then slides into the tube as one unit.

Each end is sealed with a 3D-printed end cap carrying two O-rings in grooves. The O-rings press against the inside wall of the tube to form a radial seal. Getting that seal right took about 15 iterations of the end cap design, tested in water until the hull stayed completely dry. Each end cap also has three pass-throughs:

  • Front: the tether cable to the surface buoy, the front ballast tank's water line, and the depth sensor.
  • Back: the servo cable, the rear ballast tank's water line, and the thruster power cable.

Cables and ballast tubes pass through watertight bulkhead connectors. Each one seals against the end cap with a nut and rubber washer, and a compression nut on the other side grips the cable or tube. The depth sensor threads into a brass bulkhead fitting, sealed with O-rings and PTFE thread tape, so only its sensing face touches the water. Multi-conductor cables are potted with epoxy where they're cut, so water can't wick down the inside of the cable into the hull.

Structure and assembly

The outer shell is printed in segments that join with press-fit metal pins. The end segments have eight M4 heat-set threaded inserts each. On the front, M4 bolts pass through the nose and the front end cap and thread into the first shell segment. On the back, they pass through the thruster tail, the servo bay, and the back end cap and thread into the last segment. This clamps everything together and gives the end caps a second, mechanical way to stay in, even though water pressure and O-ring friction already hold them in place.

Ballast and trim

Each of the two ballast tanks is a 200 mL syringe. A NEMA 17 stepper motor drives a T8 lead screw with an anti-backlash nut, moving a custom 3D-printed plunger to draw water in (dive) or push it out (surface). The steppers' precision lets the sub be tuned very close to neutral buoyancy.

To keep the plunger from running past its travel, each one carries a small neodymium magnet, and A3144 Hall-effect sensors on the outside of the syringe act as end stops. The TMC2209 stepper drivers' StallGuard stall detection is a backup, not the primary limit: electrical noise from the thruster controller and falling battery voltage could make it unreliable.

To stay level, the center of mass has to sit directly below the center of buoyancy. With one tank in the front and one in the back, the sub can shift water between them to correct pitch. For example, if neutral buoyancy takes 100 mL total and the nose points up, it might carry 75 mL in front and 25 mL in back. A self-leveling routine reads the IMU and adjusts the tanks automatically.

Steering

Four 3D-printed fins at the tail steer the vehicle, each driven by its own metal-gear servo. The servos sit in the flooded tail, so I opened each one and waterproofed it with marine grease, marine epoxy, and an O-ring to keep water out. They sit just ahead of the ducted thruster so the water it draws in flows over them, which keeps them effective even at low speed. The fins use a profile I designed based on a slightly modified NACA 0012 foil. A symmetric foil works equally well pushing the nose up or down, and it stalls less and runs smoother than a flat plate.

Power and electronics

I built the battery pack from 24 Molicel P42A 21700 cells (4200 mAh, 45 A each) in a 6S4P layout, spot-welded with nickel strip. I did all of the spot welding and soldered the battery management system's connections to the pack myself. It supplies about 22–24 V and up to 180 A, far more current than the sub needs, chosen for runtime. A 6S battery management system balances the six cell groups and protects against over-charge and over-discharge.

Power is split into three levels:

  • 24 V straight from the pack, for the main thruster.
  • 12 V through a buck converter, for the ballast stepper motors.
  • 5 V through a second buck converter, for the microcontrollers and sensors.

The main thruster is a 500 W brushless underwater thruster with a ducted propeller. It runs on 12–24 V and produces 23.6 lb of thrust at 24 V. It's driven by a 60 A waterproof, water-cooled electronic speed controller rated for 2–6S packs, whose built-in 8 A regulator can also supply 5–6 V to other electronics.

Arduino microcontrollers handle about 25 inputs and outputs, with room for more as sensors are added. The sensors include:

  • An external pressure sensor to measure depth.
  • An MPU-6050 IMU to measure pitch and roll for self-leveling and to tune fin control. It can also tell whether the sub is sinking or rising.
  • A BME280 sensor inside the hull that watches the air pressure. A sudden change means a leak.
  • Four Hall-effect sensors as the ballast end stops.
  • A NEO-6M GPS module on the surface buoy for position.

Testing and next steps

  • Watertight hull: After about 15 end cap iterations, the final design sealed completely in water testing, with no leaks into the hull.
  • Ballast system: I bench-tested both ballast tanks with an Arduino driving the TMC2209 stepper drivers. The Hall-effect sensors worked reliably as contactless limit switches, stopping each plunger at the ends of its travel.
  • Battery pack: Built, spot-welded, and wired to its battery management system.

The next step is dynamic testing: running the full vehicle over longer distances once I have access to a suitable test environment. After that, Phase 2 adds autonomous navigation along a preset path. Phase 3 fills the nose bay with environmental sensors (water temperature, dissolved solids, turbidity, and dissolved oxygen) along with data logging and transmission.
